The Riemann Hypothesis in pictures

Twelve figures you can play with: what the conjecture says, why primes and complex zeros are two views of one object, and how the proof announced on 20 November 2026 is reported to work.

Where things stand on 22 November 2026

Checked by machine
On 20 November Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a four-page note saying he believes that, working with Claude, he has proved the Riemann hypothesis and its generalisation to Dirichlet L-functions. The proof is a Lean library of about 17 million lines in 175,187 files. On 21 November a joint statement from the Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, certified both the statement and the proof: the main theorem is Mathlib's own definition RiemannHypothesis, several independent proof checkers accept it, and it rests only on Lean's three standard axioms.
Not yet readable
There is no paper. The informal write-up is said to run past 250,000 pages and has not been released. Alpöge says he intends to simplify and explain the argument, and that some of the automorphic machinery Claude brought in lies outside his own expertise. Number theorists have only begun to read the code.
Open questions
People auditing the code have found internal definitions that carry less content than their names suggest, and an unreviewed paper argues from this that the proof is invalid. A participant in one verification project who logged the same pattern says it does not make the kernel-checked theorem invalid; it bears on what the intermediate statements mean. A separate 940-page human-written proof of GRH by Alessandro Masullo was released the same day; gaps were reported within hours and it is under revision.

Sections 1 to 9 are established mathematics. Sections 10 and 11 describe the announced proof from the outside, using the note and a reading guide by early readers of the code. Treat them as a first map.

Part IThe problem

1Primes thin out smoothly, and jitter

Write π(x) for the number of primes up to x. Gauss noticed as a teenager that near x about one number in ln x is prime. Adding up those densities gives a prediction:

π(x) ≈ Li(x) = ∫2x dtln t

The prime number theorem (1896) proves that the ratio of the two sides tends to 1. The Riemann hypothesis concerns what is left over. It says the error is about as small as it could possibly be: of order √x.

Worked example

At x = 106 there are 78,498 primes and Li(x) ≈ 78,627. The miss is 129, while √x = 1,000. If the hypothesis holds, |π(x) − li(x)| < √x · ln x / 8π for every x ≥ 2,657 (Schoenfeld, 1976).

2Euler's product

For s > 1 Euler found that a sum over all integers equals a product over all primes:

ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 1ns = ∏p 11 − p−s

Expand each factor as a geometric series 1 + p−s + p−2s + ⋯ and multiply out. Every n appears exactly once, because every integer factors into primes in exactly one way. The product is unique factorisation written as analysis.

Two consequences. A convergent product of non-zero factors is non-zero, so ζ has no zeros with Re s > 1. Everything interesting happens further left, where the product stops converging.

3ζ on the complex plane

Riemann's move was to let s = σ + it be complex. Each term becomes an arrow:

n−s = n−σ · e−it ln n

Its length is n−σ and its angle is −t ln n, so as t grows the arrows spin at different speeds. Analytic continuation extends ζ to every s ≠ 1, and ζ(s) is where the arrows end up. A zero is a value of s at which they cancel exactly.

The continued function has a mirror symmetry. With ξ(s) = ½s(s − 1)π−s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s), one has ξ(s) = ξ(1 − s), so zeros are symmetric about the line σ = ½. There are easy zeros at −2, −4, −6, … All the others lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1.

Riemann hypothesisEvery zero of ζ in the strip 0 < σ < 1 has σ = ½.

Before this month the evidence was large and incomplete. Hardy proved in 1914 that infinitely many zeros lie on the line. Computers have confirmed the hypothesis for every zero up to height 3 × 1012. Conrey showed in 1989 that more than 40% of zeros are on the line, and in August 2026 a Lean-checked result by Alpöge and Ralph Furman at Anthropic raised that to more than 67%.

4Zeros are the frequencies of the primes

Count primes with weights: let ψ(x) be the sum of ln p over all prime powers pk ≤ x. The prime number theorem says ψ(x) ≈ x. Riemann's explicit formula, proved by von Mangoldt, is exact:

ψ(x) = x − ∑ρ xρρ − ln 2π − ½ ln(1 − x−2)

The sum runs over the zeros ρ in the strip. Pair a zero ρ = β + iγ with its conjugate and together they contribute one wave:

− 2xβ|ρ| cos(γ ln x − arg ρ)

The height γ of the zero is the pitch of the wave. Its real part β sets the loudness, which grows like xβ.

Worked example

The prime powers up to 10 are 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, so ψ(10) = 3 ln 2 + 2 ln 3 + ln 5 + ln 7 = ln 2520 ≈ 7.83. The number 2520 is the least common multiple of 1, …, 10. In general ψ(n) = ln lcm(1, …, n).

5Why ½: the square-root law

A zero with real part β produces an error of size xβ. Zeros come in mirror pairs β and 1 − β, so the best one can hope for is that every β equals ½. Then every wave has amplitude √x, and

ψ(x) = x + O(√x ln2 x)

This statement is equivalent to the hypothesis. It is the fluctuation you get from coin flips: the running total of N fair ±1 tosses wanders about √N from zero. In those terms the hypothesis says the primes are as regular as a random-looking sequence can be. The same claim in olympiad dress: the Möbius sum μ(1) + ⋯ + μ(x) is O(x½+ε).

6The zeros look like a spectrum

Rescale the gaps between consecutive zeros so that the average gap is 1. They do not look like gaps between randomly scattered points. They look like gaps between eigenvalues of large random Hermitian matrices: the zeros repel one another. Montgomery found this in 1973 and Odlyzko's computations confirmed it to high accuracy.

This is the best evidence for an old idea of Hilbert and Pólya: the heights γ are the eigenvalues of some self-adjoint operator. Such eigenvalues are real, which would be the hypothesis. Connes's trace-formula programme follows this route, and so does the Masullo manuscript. The announced proof takes a different road, and to see it we have to visit a parallel world.

Part IIA world where it is a theorem

7Curves over finite fields

Replace the integers by polynomials over the finite field Fp, and number fields by curves. A curve C of genus g has a zeta function built from its point counts Nk = #C(Fpk):

Z(T) = exp(∑k≥1 Nk Tkk) = P(T)(1 − T)(1 − pT)

Here P(T) = (1 − α1T) ⋯ (1 − α2gT) is a polynomial of degree 2g. The Riemann hypothesis for C says |αi| = √p. Substitute T = p−s and this reads: all zeros have Re s = ½. The critical line has become a circle. Hasse proved it for elliptic curves in 1933 and Weil for all curves in the 1940s. Its practical meaning is a square-root error once more:

|N1 − (p + 1)| ≤ 2g√p

Worked example

Take y2 = x3 − x over F5. The solutions are (0, 0), (1, 0), (4, 0), (2, ±1), (3, ±2). With the point at infinity, N = 8, so a5 = 6 − 8 = −2 and α = −1 ± 2i. Indeed |α|2 = 5.

8Stepanov's trick: make zeros expensive

Weil's proof needs the geometry of the surface C × C. In 1969 Stepanov found an elementary proof for a large class of curves, which Schmidt and Bombieri extended to all of them. It rests on a fact every olympiad student knows:

Degree boundA non-zero polynomial of degree D has at most D roots, counted with multiplicity.

To bound the number N of points, build a non-zero auxiliary polynomial that vanishes to order at least m at every one of them, while keeping its degree D under control. Then N · m ≤ D, so N ≤ D/m.

The auxiliary polynomial is never written down. Its existence comes from linear algebra: arrange for more free coefficients than vanishing conditions. High-order vanishing is tested with derivatives, and in characteristic p derivatives are generous, since (xp)′ = 0.

The result has the shape N ≤ main term + C · (p/m + m), and the best choice is m ≈ √p. That is where the square root comes from. Only an upper bound comes out, and that is enough: for y2 = f(x) the same argument bounds the x where f(x) is a non-square, the two counts add up to about p, and so an upper bound for each is a lower bound for the other.

9The missing ingredient over the integers

Number theorists keep a dictionary between polynomials over Fp and integers.

Polynomials Fp[t]Integers Z
a polynomial fan integer n
a point a (the factor t − a)a prime p
f vanishes to order m at apm divides n
the degree of fthe size ln |n|
roots with multiplicity ≤ degree∑p vp(n) ln p = ln |n|

The last row is unique factorisation again: an integer cannot be divisible by more than its size allows. Chebyshev's bounds on primes around 1850 run on exactly this, and the version with a binomial coefficient is an olympiad classic. The number C(2n, n) is an integer smaller than 4n, and every prime between n and 2n divides it. Hence

∑n<p≤2n ln p ≤ ln C(2n, n) < 2n ln 2

an upper bound for a sum over primes, obtained from divisibility on one side and size on the other.

Chebyshev's bound is off by a constant factor. To reach the Riemann hypothesis the error has to come down to √x, and Stepanov's recipe then asks for vanishing of order m ≈ √x. Over Fp[t] you get high order by differentiating. An integer has no derivative. For half a century that was where the transfer stopped.

Part IIIThe announced proof

10Multiplicity from a chain of theta lifts

Reported, not yet digested. This section is assembled from Alpöge's four-page note and from a reading guide by early readers of the Lean code, who state that the central steps are read off the code and the announcement and have not been re-derived. Expect corrections.

The note describes the argument as a descendant of Stepanov's, in which each theta lift mimics one unit of multiplicity. The guide gives this dictionary:

Stepanov, curves over FpAnnounced proof, over Z
vanishing to order mdivisibility of an m-fold iterated theta lift
counting degreesBost's slope inequality
the finite extensions of Fpthe layers of a cyclotomic tower of fields
upper bounds sufficea theorem of Landau type

What is a theta lift? A theta function such as ϑ(z) = ∑ eπin²z packages a lattice into a highly symmetric function. The theta correspondence uses such a function as a kernel that carries automorphic forms from one group to a partner group. The groups here are unitary groups U(Xj) of positive-definite Hermitian spaces. On those, an automorphic form is just a function on a finite set of lattice classes, so a lift is a finite matrix with arithmetic entries, and the output of one lift can be fed into the next.

Why would that create multiplicity? The guide's model case is an elliptic curve with complex multiplication by Q(√−d), with theta function ϑ and period Ω. The normalised derivatives Daϑ(0)/Ωa are divisible by p to the power a/p + a/p2 + ⋯ − O(log a) at every prime p that stays prime in that field. That exponent is Legendre's formula for the power of p in a!. Differentiating a theta function manufactures factorial-sized divisibility at many primes at once. The chain replaces “differentiate m times” by “lift m times”.

Put together, the guide's outline runs like this.

  1. TargetA smoothed sum over prime powers, G(x) = ∑n≤x Λ(n)(x/n − 1) − x(ln x − 1 − γ), and its twists by Dirichlet characters. By the explicit formula it is a sum of waves xβ over zeros. Goal: G(x) ≤ x½+ε on windows [Y, Y3/2] for arbitrarily large Y.
  2. Auxiliary objectThe composite Θ = Θm−1 ∘ ⋯ ∘ Θ0 of theta lifts, each twisted by a Hecke character. It plays the part of Stepanov's polynomial.
  3. Finite primesΘ is integral, and at inert primes it is more divisible than integrality alone would give. The total gain is a weighted sum of ln p, which is a sum over primes.
  4. The infinite placeThe size of Θ is computed with classical exact formulas: Chowla and Selberg's for the period Ω, Siegel's mass formula, and the Rallis inner product formula.
  5. Slope inequalityBost's inequality is the arithmetic form of the degree bound: divisibility at the finite primes has to be paid for by size at infinity. Out comes an upper bound for the prime sum with error about x/m + m + √x.
  6. LandauA one-sided bound this strong leaves no room for a zero with real part above ½. Figure 5 shows why: its wave would swing up through the ceiling. The functional equation reflects the left half of the strip onto the right, so every zero is on the line. Running the argument for each Dirichlet character gives the generalised hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions.

Who did what. By Alpöge's account he had aimed at this problem since his student days and steered the work toward a setting modelled on a simpler toy problem. He credits Claude with proposing the chain of lifts and with outpacing him near the end. The note's research timeline, from 6 October to 16 November, was compiled by Claude, and he warns that it is rough. The note does not say which Claude model was used.

11What a machine check settles

Lean's kernel checks one thing: that a proof term has the type it claims. Here the type is Mathlib's RiemannHypothesis, a definition written by other people years earlier. Two comparison tools confirmed that the release proves that exact statement. Independent kernels re-checked the full proof, and an axiom audit found only propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound. That settles whether the formal statement follows from the axioms, as far as the checkers themselves can be trusted.

It leaves two things open. One is whether every definition underneath says what mathematicians mean by it; Mathlib's statement is short and widely used, which helps. The other is the why. A proof nobody can read cannot be taught, shortened or extended. Terence Tao's first reading is that the argument combines recent results from the Langlands programme and arithmetic geometry and will take time to absorb. James Maynard told Nature he suspects it will turn out to be correct. Alpöge expects much shorter proofs to follow.

12If it stands

Decades of theorems begin with the words “assume GRH”. For Dirichlet L-functions they would lose that clause.

  • Prime counting with square-root error: |π(x) − li(x)| < √x ln x / 8π for x ≥ 2,657.
  • Primes spread just as evenly among the residue classes of any modulus.
  • Miller's primality test becomes a proven deterministic algorithm: checking bases up to 2 ln2 n is enough (Bach).

The method may matter more than the statement. If the chain of lifts really does the work of a derivative of an integer, it is a new tool, and the obvious next targets are the L-functions the announcement does not cover.
